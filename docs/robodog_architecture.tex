\begin{abstract}\noindent\small
This work presents the design, implementation and simulation evaluation of
RoboDog, a 28.000\,kg, 12-degree-of-freedom quadruped built around twelve
ROBSTRIDE06 actuators. A reproducible model pipeline transforms a CAD-derived
URDF into consistent kinematic, inertial, ROS 2 and MuJoCo descriptions. Each
knee uses a 2:1 external belt transmission with 95\,\% assumed efficiency; the
hips remain direct drive. The controller combines phase-based gait scheduling,
continuous stance and swing trajectories, Raibert-style foot placement,
stance-force distribution, inverse kinematics, joint impedance and an
unconditional safety layer at \RdControlRate\,Hz. Physics runs at 2\,kHz and
applied actuator torque is sampled every physics step. Registered RGB-D data
feed RTAB-Map graph SLAM and graph-corrected OctoMap products for persistent
room scans. In 100\,s, 1.0\,m/s flat-ground tests, walk failed stability;
heading-held trot remained upright and achieved 0.923\,m/s, with a worst
per-motor RMS torque of 6.749\,N$\cdot$m and a 34.897\,N$\cdot$m peak. Safety
limiting occurred during 87.965\,\% of trot control cycles, so the operating
point failed the tracking and motor-margin criteria. The results establish a
reproducible simulation and mapping architecture, but do not establish
hardware feasibility, validated thermal margin or reliable 1.0\,m/s motion at
28\,kg.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} quadruped robot; legged locomotion; ROS 2;
impedance control; gait generation; MuJoCo; RGB-D SLAM; RTAB-Map; OctoMap;
actuator sizing.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Quadruped robots exchange the static simplicity of wheels for discontinuous
contact, a floating body and a larger reachable set of footholds. Their
controller must decide which feet support the body, where the other feet land,
which ground-reaction forces are admissible, and which joint torques realize
those forces without exceeding actuator or thermal limits. These problems are
coupled to state estimation, rigid-body modeling and the timing behavior of the
software graph.

This report asks four system-level questions. First, can one source model keep
the CAD-derived mass properties, URDF, controller kinematics and MuJoCo plant
consistent? Second, can the present controller move the 28\,kg configuration at
1.0\,m/s within the ROBSTRIDE06 envelope? Third, can shared ROS 2 contracts
preserve command and telemetry meaning across ideal, MuJoCo and CAN backends?
Fourth, can the simulated RGB-D pipeline build and persist coherent 2D and 3D
maps? The contributions are a traceable model-generation pipeline, a
backend-independent control and safety architecture, a physics-rate actuator
load method, and an integrated SLAM/occupancy/export path.

The evidence boundary is central to every conclusion. No actuator, battery,
IMU or camera has been commissioned on the physical robot. The 28\,kg test
adds a centered point-mass ballast to a 19.719271\,kg base model, not a measured
payload inertia. MuJoCo provides ground-truth odometry. Temperature is an
uncalibrated observer output. Accordingly, this is a reproducible simulation
study and software architecture report, not hardware qualification.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:scientific-framing}

RoboDog is a torque-controlled, floating-base quadruped: four identical
three-DOF legs support and propel a rigid body through intermittent ground
contact. The gait controller is deliberately classical rather than learned:
a phase scheduler chooses stance and swing legs; a foot-trajectory generator
places feet; a body stabiliser requests ground-reaction forces; the analytic
Jacobian maps those foot forces to joint torques; and the RS06 actuator closes
its local impedance loop. This split follows Raibert's separation of legged
balance into speed, attitude and height regulation, while keeping the
implementation small enough to verify line by line~\cite{raibert,raibert-darpa}.
Modern platforms such as MIT Cheetah 3 combine high-bandwidth proprioceptive
actuation with more sophisticated model-based control. RoboDog does not
implement model-predictive or whole-body optimal control; those systems are
used here as context rather than as a description of this controller
~\cite{cheetah3-design,mpc-wbic}.

The mathematical core uses established robotics tools. Foot forces are
converted to actuator torques with the Jacobian-transpose relationship used by
operational-space control~\cite{khatib},
and joint commands are expressed as impedance setpoints: target position,
target velocity, stiffness, damping and feed-forward torque~\cite{hogan}. The controller therefore does
not command raw current or a single position target; it commands a local
dynamic relationship and then measures whether the simulated actuator effort,
safety limiting and thermal estimates remain credible.

The ROS 2 architecture is a typed graph of nodes, topics and services. ROS 2
documentation defines nodes as graph participants that publish/subscribe
topics, serve/request services and expose parameters; topics are typed
publish/subscribe streams for continuous data~\cite{ros2-nodes,ros2-topics};
and QoS policies such as reliability and durability determine whether a
publisher/subscriber pair can exchange messages~\cite{ros2-qos}.
RoboDog keeps high-rate joint control inside one node and publishes reduced
state for RViz, the web GUI and logging. \code{robot\_state\_publisher}
receives the URDF and \code{/joint\_states}, then publishes moving transforms
on \code{/tf} and fixed transforms on \code{/tf\_static}~\cite{robot-state-publisher}.

Frame conventions follow REP-103 ($x$ forward, $y$ left, $z$ up) and the
REP-105 mobile-frame separation: control owns
\code{odom}$\to$\code{base\_link}, while SLAM owns
\code{map}$\to$\code{odom}~\cite{rep103,rep105}.
This matters for mapping: RTAB-Map estimates and corrects the map trajectory,
and OctoMap stores probabilistic 3D occupancy as occupied, free and unknown
space~\cite{rtabmap-paper,rtabmap-ros,octomap}. Depth and room-scan data
use \code{sensor\_msgs/PointCloud2}, whose official message definition stores
an N-dimensional point collection as a binary blob described by fields and
permits image-organized depth clouds~\cite{pointcloud2}.

The torque measurements in this document use MuJoCo's applied actuator
generalized force. MuJoCo documents actuator force transmission into
\code{mjData.qfrc\_actuator}; sampling that field gives the torque actually
applied by the physics actuator at the joint DOF, after the command path and
model limits, rather than only the requested impedance term
~\cite{mujoco-paper,mujoco-computation}. RMS is then
computed as a time-weighted load summary, and it is reported together with
peaks, overload exposure, speed at peak and clipping. That combination is
necessary because a 100\,s RMS value can hide startup or touchdown spikes.

\subsection{Coordinate systems and conventions}
\label{sec:conventions}

\paragraph{Frames.} REP-103 throughout: $x$ forward, $y$ left, $z$ up.
\code{base\_link} is at the body geometric centre. \code{odom} is the world
frame for the estimated base pose and the test-world markers. Camera optical
frames follow the ROS optical convention ($z$ forward, $x$ right, $y$ down),
related to their body frames by $\mathrm{rpy} = (-\pi/2,\, 0,\, -\pi/2)$.
When mapping is active, control owns \code{odom}$\to$\code{base\_link}
and RTAB-Map owns \code{map}$\to$\code{odom}; the mapper does not replace
the base-state source. ROS uses wall timestamps without a \code{/clock} topic.

\paragraph{Joint naming.} \code{\{FL,FR,RL,RR\}\_\{haa,hfe,kfe\}\_joint}.
HAA is hip abduction/adduction, HFE hip flexion/extension, KFE the knee.

\begin{decision}{Joint axes are not mirrored between left and right}
Every HAA axis is $+x$ and every HFE and KFE axis is $+y$, expressed in
\code{base\_link} orientation, for all four legs. The alternative --- mirroring
the right side so that ``positive'' means abduction everywhere --- was rejected
because it makes the URDF axes differ per leg and turns every sign question into
a per-leg question.

The consequence must be remembered: \textbf{positive HAA abducts the left legs
and adducts the right legs.} Sim-to-real then reduces to one sign per joint in
\code{robstride\_bus.yaml}, not a frame convention.
\end{decision}

\paragraph{Zero and sign.} All joints at zero puts the legs straight down, feet
at longitudinal offsets $\pm(h_{\rm aa,x}+h_{\rm fe,dx})$, lateral offsets
$\pm(h_{\rm aa,y}+h_{\rm fe,dr}+d_{\rm thigh,y})$, and height
$-(L_1+L_2)$ relative to the base. Positive HFE swings the thigh
backward. Negative KFE folds the knee backward; KFE\,$=0$ is fully extended and
the upper limit, so the knee cannot hyperextend.

\begin{decision}{Named poses are specified as a base height, not joint angles}
Each foot hangs vertically below its own HFE axis. That one rule yields, at
once: identical joint angles on all four legs despite the non-mirrored axes; a
nominal support polygon of \RdFootprintL\,$\times$\,\RdFootprintW\,mm.
The whole-robot center of mass depends on payload placement and leg pose.

Placing the feet under the \emph{HAA} axes instead would need different front
and rear angles, because the HFE sits \RdHfeDx\,mm outboard of the HAA, and
changes the support lever arms and joint utilization; compare motor-side
loads through the active knee transmission.
\end{decision}

\section{Model generation and robot mathematics}

\subsection{CAD to URDF pipeline}

The Onshape export is a flat tree of 264 part-links with 12 revolute joints,
saved at an arbitrary mate pose. \code{tools/cad\_to\_model.py} turns it into a
canonical 13-body model:

\begin{enumerate}
\item forward-kinematics every part into the \code{root} frame;
\item cut the tree at the 12 revolute joints, giving 13 rigid bodies;
\item recover the pose-invariant leg geometry (link lengths, axis offsets);
\item solve the CAD mate angles against the canonical zero definition;
\item re-express every visual mesh and inertia tensor in canonical body frames;
\item substitute RS06 mass properties for the new CAD motor placeholders.
\end{enumerate}

The generation tool prints canonical-chain residuals against the active CAD.
Review these together with link masses and mesh transforms after each export;
a small kinematic residual alone does not verify self-collision clearance.

\begin{decision}{The URDF is generated, never hand-edited}
\code{robot\_parameters.yaml} is the single source of truth. The Xacro, the
MuJoCo MJCF, the inverse kinematics and this document all read it. A
hand-maintained URDF drifts from the CAD, and a drift between the model the
physics uses and the model the IK uses presents as a control bug that is very
hard to attribute.
\end{decision}

Because rotation about $y$ leaves $y$ unchanged, the lateral offset collapses to
a constant. That is what makes the inverse kinematics closed-form: the abduction
angle is fixed by the $(y,z)$ projection alone, and what remains is a planar
two-link problem. Forward kinematics from the HAA joint frame:
\[
p = R_x(q_0)\bigl(d_1 + R_y(q_1)\bigl(d_2 + R_y(q_2)\, d_3\bigr)\bigr)
\]
with $d_1 = (s_x\,\text{hfe}_{dx},\, s_y\,\text{hfe}_{dr},\, 0)$,
$d_2 = (0,\, s_y\,\text{lat},\, -L_1)$, $d_3 = (0,0,-L_2)$.

The IK returns only the knee-backward branch ($q_2 \le 0$); the mirror solution
exists mathematically but the robot cannot reach it. Round-tripping FK through
IK over the full joint range gives a worst-case position error below
$10^{-9}$\,m, and the analytic Jacobian matches central differences to
$10^{-6}$.

\subsection{Knee drive topology}

The knee actuator is mounted proximally on the upper leg and drives the knee
through a 2:1 external belt. The internal 9:1 actuator gearbox is already
included in the RS06 output specifications. With external ratio $r=2$ and
assumed efficiency $\eta=0.95$,
\[
 q_m=rq_j,\qquad \dot q_m=r\dot q_j,\qquad
 \tau_j=r\eta\tau_m,\qquad J_{j}=r^2J_{m}.
\]
The belt does not kinematically couple hip and knee angles. The gait and
Jacobian use joint coordinates; the shared transmission map converts at the
hardware/simulation boundary. Motor-side torque, not knee joint torque, drives
current and thermal estimates. Constant efficiency approximates motoring and
does not model compliance, backlash or regenerative braking.

\paragraph{Motor-side electrical model.} The published output-referred
$K_t=\RdKt$\,N$\cdot$m/A$_{\mathrm{rms}}$ provides a low-load phase-current
estimate. Knee torque is first divided by $2\times0.95$. The published
0.23\,$\Omega$ line resistance gives 0.115\,$\Omega$ under an equivalent-wye
model; the latter is a modeling conversion, not a separately specified winding
resistance. The constant-$K_t$ approximation does not reproduce the published
peak-current limit and is not a DC-bus current estimate or a verified Iq-to-RMS
conversion. Thermal resistance and heat capacity remain uncalibrated, so
steady-temperature predictions are illustrative.

\paragraph{Equivalent output inertia.} The manufacturer specifies
0.012\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$ at the actuator's low-speed output. The external 2:1
knee reduction multiplies it by four to 0.048\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$. This includes
the internal drive's equivalent inertia, not a direct bare-rotor measurement.
Dividing by the internal ratio squared would only infer an equivalent rotor-side
quantity. Validate assembly dynamics and friction without discarding the
published output inertia.

\subsection{CAN interface}

Extended 29-bit identifiers at \RdCanRate\,Mbit/s. The identifier carries
\texttt{[28:24] comm type | [23:8] data | [7:0] target}. The motion-control
frame (type 1) is the MIT-style impedance command: target angle, velocity, $k_p$
and $k_d$ occupy the eight payload bytes, and the feed-forward torque rides in
the identifier's data field because the payload is full.

\begin{decision}{The command interface is the actuator's native impedance frame}
\code{JointCommand} carries $(\text{position},\ \text{velocity},\ \text{effort},\
k_p,\ k_d)$ --- exactly one CAN frame. A narrower interface, such as position
only, would force the host to close a loop the motor already closes in firmware
at tens of kHz. The host sets \emph{setpoints}; it is not the servo.
\end{decision}

\subsection{Bus budget}

An extended-ID frame with eight data bytes is 131 bits nominal and up to 159
with worst-case stuffing; \RdBitsPerFrame\ bits is the design figure. Each joint
needs one command and one feedback frame per cycle.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Buses} & \textbf{Motors/bus} & \textbf{Rate} & \textbf{Load} & \textbf{Verdict} \\
\midrule
1 & 12 & 250\,Hz & 90\,\% & too tight, no margin \\
1 & 12 & 400\,Hz & 144\,\% & impossible \\

2 & 6  & 500\,Hz & 90\,\% & rejected, no margin \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\begin{decision}{Two buses at \RdControlRate\,Hz, split front/rear}
\RdControlRate\,Hz is not a compromise on control quality: because the RS06
closes its position and velocity loop in firmware, the host rate is a
\emph{setpoint update} rate. The same rate is used in simulation so that gains
tuned in MuJoCo transfer without change.

The split is front/rear rather than left/right so that each bus carries a
complete leg \emph{pair}: a single bus failure leaves the robot in a
recognisable posture rather than a diagonal collapse.
\code{RobStride06Backend.check\_bus\_budget()} refuses any configuration above
80\,\% at start-up.
\end{decision}

\section{Locomotion and trajectory generation}

\archfig{control_architecture}{Control layers. Rates fall by roughly an order of
magnitude per layer, and the innermost loop is not on the host at all.}{0.98}

\subsection{The loop}

One thread, \RdControlRate\,Hz, in \code{robodog\_control\_node}:

\begin{center}\small
\code{read()} $\to$ estimate $\to$ active controller $\to$ safety $\to$ \code{write()} $\to$ \code{step()}
\end{center}

ROS callbacks run on a separate executor and only ever set fields the loop
reads; nothing in a callback touches the backend. Publishing is decimated ---
\code{RobotState} at 50\,Hz, \code{/joint\_states} at 100\,Hz --- because
publishing telemetry at \RdControlRate\,Hz would cost more than the control it
reports on.

\archfig{data_flow}{One \RdControlRate\,Hz cycle. The safety monitor is
unconditional.}{1.0}

\begin{decision}{Controllers are selected, not composed}
Exactly one of \{\code{idle}, \code{joint}, \code{pose}, \code{gait},
\code{joint\_test}\} drives the joints at any moment. Making that exclusive by
construction is worth more than the flexibility of a controller chain, because
two things writing to the same actuator is a failure mode with no good recovery.
\end{decision}

\subsection{Impedance control}

The actuator evaluates
\[
\tau = k_p\,(q^{*} - q) + k_d\,(\dot q^{*} - \dot q) + \tau_{\mathrm{ff}}
\]
in firmware. Effective joint inertia includes the published equivalent actuator
output inertia, reflected through the external belt, plus configuration-dependent
link inertia. For an identified effective inertia $J$, the local linear estimates
are $\omega_n=\sqrt{k_p/J}$ and $k_{d,\mathrm{critical}}=2\sqrt{k_pJ}$.
The knee's actuator contribution is four times that of a direct-drive hip, so
one old RS02 inertia calculation cannot characterize every current joint.

Swing leg              & 60  & 1.5 & tracks the unloaded trajectory \\

\subsection{Finite joint-space trajectories}

Named poses use a fifth-order minimum-jerk blend rather than a position step.
For normalized time $s=\operatorname{clip}(t/T,0,1)$,
\[
  h(s)=10s^3-15s^4+6s^5,\qquad
  q^*(t)=q_0+h(s)(q_1-q_0).
\]
Because $h'(0)=h'(1)=h''(0)=h''(1)=0$, the transition begins and ends with
zero target velocity and acceleration. The greeting is a simulation-only
whole-body motion with its own support-polygon check and recovery to stand. It
is an operator demonstration, not a locomotion gait or evidence of hardware
stability.

\subsection{Gravity feed-forward and the force convention}

Each stance leg is fed the torque that holds its share of the body weight. The
sign convention is stated once and used everywhere:

\begin{center}
$f$ is the force the \emph{environment} applies to the foot (the ground
reaction, $+z$ in stance), so \quad
$\tau_{\mathrm{actuator}} = -J^{\mathsf{T}} f$, \quad
$f = -(J^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}\tau_{\mathrm{actuator}}$.
\end{center}

\begin{caveat}{This sign is not cosmetic}
An inverted feed-forward makes a stance leg push the body \emph{down}. Measured
in MuJoCo: with correct feed-forward the body sags 0.31\,mm below the commanded
\RdStandHeight\,mm; without feed-forward it sags 5.34\,mm; with the sign
inverted it sags 11.95\,mm. Magnitude-only tests pass in all three cases, which
is why \code{test\_kinematics.py} asserts the \emph{direction} of the knee
torque explicitly.
\end{caveat}

\subsection{Gait generation}

A single normalised cycle phase advances at the step frequency. Each leg has a
fixed offset and is in stance for \code{duty\_factor} of its period.
Diagonal pairing produces a trot, lateral pairing a pace, and fore/hind pairing
a bound, consistent with the classical quadruped gait taxonomy
~\cite{raibert-gaits}.

\subsubsection{What the generator actually does}

The gait generator is a \emph{foot trajectory planner}. It does not compute
torques and it does not know what a motor is. Once per control cycle it is
given the elapsed time and the body's measured state, and it returns, for each
of the four legs, where that foot should be, how fast it should be going, and
how hard it should push. Everything else --- inverse kinematics, the impedance
law, the actuator --- happens downstream.

Concretely, \code{GaitGenerator.update(dt, feedback)} returns twelve joint
positions, twelve joint velocities, twelve feed-forward torques, and a
four-element contact flag. The joint values come from running each foot
position through the closed-form leg IK; the torques come from the balance
layer. A gait is therefore defined entirely by \emph{geometry and timing}, and
adding one is a block in \code{gaits.yaml} plus a phase offset --- no new code.

\archfig{gait_pipeline}{One control cycle of the gait generator. The
generator plans feet, not torques: everything it returns is a position, a
velocity or a force, and the impedance law runs two layers below. Continuity
is the invariant --- stance resumes from the last commanded position and swing
starts from where stance left the foot, so the command cannot step at either
transition.}{1.0}

\subsubsection{The phase model}

One normalised phase $\phi \in [0,1)$ advances at the step frequency and wraps.
Each leg $i$ reads the same clock through its own offset:
\[
  \phi_i = (\phi + \text{offset}_i) \bmod 1,
  \qquad
  \text{leg } i \text{ is in \textsc{stance} iff } \phi_i < D
\]
where $D$ is the duty factor. That single line is what distinguishes a walk
from a trot: the offsets decide which feet are down together, and $D$ decides
for how long. A duty of 0.75 with the walk offsets always leaves three feet
down, so the robot is statically stable; a duty of 0.5 with the trot offsets
leaves two, on a diagonal, so it is not.

\subsubsection{Stance: the foot is a fixed point in the world}

A planted foot does not move. It follows that in the \emph{body} frame it must
translate backwards at exactly the body's velocity --- if the body advances
10\,mm, the foot must appear 10\,mm further back, or the command is asking the
leg to drag the robot through the contact.

\[
  \mathbf{p}^{\text{stance}}_i \mathrel{-}= \mathbf{v}\,\delta t,
  \qquad
  \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{meas}}
    + g_{\text{sweep}}\,(\mathbf{v}^{*} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{meas}})
\]

The sweep gain $g_{\text{sweep}}$ chooses which velocity to believe, and the
default of 1.0 --- the \emph{commanded} velocity --- is the least obvious
decision in the whole generator. It looks like it must scuff. In steady state
it does not: once the body is travelling at the commanded speed, the target
sweeps at exactly the rate the planted foot is already moving through the body
frame, and the relative motion is zero. When the body is \emph{not} at speed,
the target runs ahead of the foot and the leg impedance turns that gap into a
tangential force.

That is the point. Sweeping at the commanded velocity is proportional velocity
feedback routed through the leg, and it is the dominant thing regulating speed.
Sweeping at the measured velocity instead is self-consistent at any speed,
including the wrong one: measured at $g_{\text{sweep}} = 0$, a walk commanded
to 0.15\,m/s free-ran to 0.272\,m/s and then fell over.

\subsubsection{Swing: interpolate between two latched endpoints}

Swing carries the foot from where it left the ground to where it should next
land. Both endpoints are explicit positions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathbf{p}_0$ is latched at lift-off --- wherever stance actually left
    the foot, not where it was supposed to be;
  \item $\mathbf{p}_1$ is the landing point, tracked continuously through the
    swing so a foot in the air still reacts to the body accelerating.
\end{itemize}

\[
  \mathbf{p}^{\text{swing}}(u)
    = \mathbf{p}_0 + (\mathbf{p}_1 - \mathbf{p}_0)\,\sigma(u),
  \qquad
  \sigma(u) = u - \frac{\sin 2\pi u}{2\pi}
\]

$\sigma'(u) = 1 - \cos 2\pi u$ vanishes at both ends, so the foot leaves and
arrives with no horizontal velocity relative to the ground --- it is placed,
not dragged. The vertical profile $\sin^2(\pi u)$ is likewise zero-slope at
both ends, with a small \code{touchdown\_depth} subtracted late in swing so the
foot seeks the floor rather than hovering over it, and decayed again over the
first sixth of stance so that seeking does not itself become a step.

Interpolating between two latched endpoints is what makes the command
continuous, and it is worth being explicit about why that is not a detail.
Section~\ref{sec:locomotion-status} records what happened when it was not.

\subsubsection{Foot placement: where velocity is really decided}

Attitude control holds a robot up; it cannot make it go anywhere. A legged
robot regulates velocity by \emph{where it puts its feet}. The landing point
uses the Raibert heuristic:
\[
  \mathbf{p}_1 = \mathbf{p}_{\text{nom}}
    + \underbrace{\mathbf{v}_{\text{meas}}\,\frac{T_{\text{stance}}}{2}}_{\text{neutral point}}
    + \underbrace{k_v\,(\mathbf{v}_{\text{meas}} - \mathbf{v}^{*})}_{\text{correction}}
\]
The first term is the landing point that \emph{sustains} the current velocity:
land half a stance-sweep ahead, sweep back a full stance-sweep, end half behind,
and the stride is symmetric about the hip. The second term breaks that symmetry
deliberately --- landing further ahead than neutral decelerates, further behind
accelerates. The offset is clamped to \code{max\_placement\_m} so a large
velocity error cannot ask for a footfall outside the workspace.

\subsubsection{Three things regulate speed, and they are not interchangeable}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.42\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Acts through} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
Stance sweep     & leg impedance & Dominant. Turns velocity error into
                                   tangential force every cycle. \\
Tangential wrench & feed-forward torque & Explicit force request,
                                   $k_{p,v}(\mathbf{v}^{*}-\mathbf{v})$,
                                   friction-cone limited. \\

                                   \emph{at}; acts once per step, not
                                   continuously. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Removing any one degrades tracking; removing the first breaks it outright.

\subsubsection{Force distribution}

Positions alone do not hold a robot up. The contact flag from the phase model
tells the balance layer which feet may push, and it solves for the ground
reaction at each of them that best reproduces the desired body wrench
(Section~\ref{sec:balance}). Those forces become feed-forward joint torques
through $\boldsymbol{\tau} = -J^{\top}\mathbf{f}$. The gait generator supplies
the geometry and the contact schedule; the balance layer supplies the forces;
neither one alone walks.

\subsubsection{Per-cycle summary}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Advance $\phi$; for each leg compute $\phi_i$ and its stance/swing state.
  \item Compute the Raibert landing offset from measured and commanded velocity.
  \item \textsc{Stance}: on touchdown, resume from the last commanded position;
        retract at the sweep velocity; decay the touchdown depth.
  \item \textsc{Swing}: on lift-off, latch $\mathbf{p}_0$; interpolate
        $\mathbf{p}_0 \to \mathbf{p}_1$ with the zero-endpoint-velocity profile
        and the $\sin^2$ lift.
  \item Run each foot position and velocity through the leg IK and Jacobian to
        get $q$ and $\dot q$.
  \item Ask the balance layer for the body wrench, distribute it over the
        stance feet, convert to feed-forward torque.
  \item Return $q$, $\dot q$, $\tau_{\text{ff}}$, contact.
\end{enumerate}

Stride length is never configured, and there is no stride parameter to
configure. Swing interpolates between where the foot left the ground and where
placement says it should land, so the stride is whatever those two points
imply. The workspace cap is \code{max\_placement\_m}, which bounds the landing
offset directly --- 0.12\,m against a 0.43\,m leg, comfortably inside reach.

\subsection{Balance}
\label{sec:balance}

A foot-trajectory generator alone does not walk. With the open-loop gait and no
balance term, a 0.3\,m/s trot in MuJoCo reached 25$^\circ$ of body tilt,
saturated the actuators at the \RdTorquePeak\,N$\cdot$m peak and travelled
28\,mm in six seconds. The feet were following their commanded paths; the body
was not being held up. \code{balance.py} adds the two things that were missing.

\paragraph{Force distribution.} A PD controller on height, roll, pitch, yaw rate
and horizontal velocity produces a desired body wrench, and the stance foot
forces are solved to reproduce it:
\[
\sum_i f_i = F, \qquad \sum_i r_i \times f_i = M
\]
Six equations in $3 n_{\mathrm{stance}}$ unknowns --- exactly determined for a
trot, under-determined for a walk or a full stance --- solved by least squares,
then projected into what a contact can deliver: $f_z \ge 0$ and
$\lVert f_{xy} \rVert \le \mu f_z$.

\begin{decision}{A stance leg is force controlled, not position controlled}
This was measured the hard way. With \code{stance\_kp} at 120 the stance legs
showed 0.17\,rad of tracking error and 20\,N$\cdot$m of pure disagreement with
reality: the commanded foot position was advancing at the \emph{commanded} body
velocity while the foot was physically planted, so the leg was dragging the
robot through its own contact. Two changes follow. The stance foot now
translates at a blend of the \emph{measured} and commanded velocity
(\code{stance\_sweep\_gain}), and the placement offset is latched at touchdown
so the command cannot move under a planted foot. Stiffness is reduced to 90:
raising it turns the leg into a spring that anchors the body to its own foot
and cancels the propulsion force.
\end{decision}

\paragraph{Foot placement.} Attitude control cannot regulate velocity --- a
legged robot steers by where it puts its feet. The Raibert heuristic places the
landing point at $v\,T_{\mathrm{stance}}/2 + k_v (v - v^{*})$.

\subsubsection{How it was broken, and what that cost}

For most of this project the robot did not travel. It stepped in place and
drifted backwards at roughly 0.04\,m/s, while every travelling gait drew 141
to 201\,\% of the continuous torque rating. Both symptoms had the same two
causes, and neither was in the balance layer or the gains, which is where the
effort went first.

\paragraph{A 41\,mm step at every lift-off.} Stance retracts the foot at the
sweep velocity, but the swing arc was rebuilt from scratch each cycle as
$\mathbf{p}_{\text{nom}} + \text{stride}\,(\sigma - \tfrac12)$ --- which
assumes stance had swept the foot back by the full \emph{commanded} stride.
Whenever the robot was not already at the commanded speed the two disagreed,
and the difference was commanded in a single 2.5\,ms tick at every lift-off:
41\,mm from a standstill at 0.30\,m/s. That step drove 0.33\,rad of tracking
error, which saturated the hips at the \RdTorquePeak\,N$\cdot$m peak and so
destroyed the force distribution the balance layer had just computed. The robot
could not accelerate, so the measured velocity stayed at zero, so the step
never shrank. The gait fought itself into a steady state.

\paragraph{Two jammed hips.} The thigh capsule and the HAA actuator cylinder
are both conservative bounding volumes around parts that clear each other in
the CAD, and MuJoCo does not auto-filter the pair because the thigh is the
base's \emph{grandchild} (base $\to$ hip $\to$ thigh), not its child. The
front hips therefore jammed 13\,mrad above the standing pose and would not
move under 16.6\,N$\cdot$m, while the rear hips swung 1.59\,rad on 3\,N$\cdot$m.
The front legs could only lift with the knee, and the hip absorbed the entire
impedance error as heat. The robot was, in the most literal sense, walking with
two of its twelve joints welded shut.

\begin{caveat}{A careful measurement of a broken system is still a measurement of a broken system}
The stance-height study in \code{gaits.yaml} was repeated at four heights, was
internally consistent, and was wrong --- it concluded that the balance layer
stopped helping above 340\,mm, which was an artifact of the jam. So was the
finding that lower stance impedance reduced torque: with the hips free, the
original \code{stance\_kp} of 90 turned out to be the best value tested. The
tuning was never the problem, and no amount of it would have helped.

What eventually found both bugs was not more tuning but per-joint
instrumentation: printing commanded against measured position for one leg
through one gait cycle showed a 40\,mm jump in the target at lift-off, and a
hip angle that did not change by 0.001\,rad across four seconds under peak
torque. A joint that will not move under 16\,N$\cdot$m is not a control
problem.
\end{caveat}

Both are now covered by tests that fail if they return:
\code{test\_the\_foot\_command\_never\_jumps} asserts the commanded foot
position never moves more than 5\,mm in one tick, at four different measured
velocities, and
\code{test\_every\_hip\_can\_swing\_through\_its\_commanded\_range} drives each
hip alone and requires all four to move, and to move alike.

\subsubsection{What the balance layer is for, now}

Fixing the trajectory changed the answer. The balance layer used to be what
kept a 0.3\,m/s trot from reaching 25$^\circ$ of tilt; with the swing arc
continuous and the hips free, the open-loop gait is well behaved on its own and
the two are within a few tenths of a degree --- 5.1$^\circ$ with balance
against 4.9$^\circ$ without. Tilt is no longer where the layer earns its place.

Course holding is. Over six seconds at 0.5\,m/s the lateral drift is 0.054\,m
with balance and 0.299\,m without, because the Raibert term is the only thing
steering the feet back under the body, and velocity tracking degrades from
$-0.007$ to $-0.018$\,m/s. A robot that wanders 0.3\,m sideways in six seconds
does not get through a doorway. The regression test asserts those two things
rather than tilt.

resistance; the friction and damping are estimates. Treat 90\,\% as
``plausibly inside the rating'', not as a measurement of a real motor.
\end{caveat}

\subsection{State estimation}

\begin{decision}{A complementary filter and leg odometry, not an EKF}
The estimator module implements the intended sensor-fusion algorithm, but its
real IMU input is not integrated into the control loop. Hardware travel is
blocked while base feedback is unavailable. Given actual sensor input, attitude
comes from blending gyro integration with the gravity direction, with
the gravity term weighted by how closely the measured acceleration magnitude
resembles $g$ --- during a hard push-off it does not. Height and velocity come
from treating each foot in contact as a temporary fixed point.

An EKF was rejected \emph{for now} because until the robot exists its process
and measurement covariances would be invented numbers. This is honest,
debuggable; it still requires sensor integration and physical validation. Yaw is not observable
from an IMU without a magnetometer and is flagged as drifting.
\end{decision}

\subsection{Two-tier limits}

Torque and speed values in this table are actuator-output references. The knee
joint's continuous budget is 15.2\,N$\cdot$m and peak is 68.4\,N$\cdot$m
with the assumed 2:1 ratio and 95\,\% efficiency; its speed limits halve.
Enforcement uses these per-joint mappings rather than one scalar joint limit.

The URDF carries modeled hard bounds, not a guarantee of sustained physical
output; the operational tier constrains requests within the configured policy.
Temperature thresholds are project policy and do not identify the feedback
sensor as a winding-temperature measurement.

\subsection{Design rules}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{It runs in simulation too.} The monitor executes on every cycle
      regardless of backend. A safety layer that only activates on hardware has
      never been tested by the time it matters.
\item \textbf{It is a pure function.} \code{(measurement, request, state)}
      $\to$ \code{(allowed command, status)}. No ROS, no I/O, no threads --- which
      is what makes the whole envelope testable. \code{test\_safety.py} is the
      specification.
\item \textbf{It clamps rather than rejects.} Dropping a cycle mid-stride is
      more dangerous than saturating one.
\item \textbf{Protective faults latch.} Clearing requires the condition to have
      gone away \emph{and} an explicit operator action.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Torque limiting under impedance control}

\begin{decision}{Scale the whole $(k_p, k_d, \tau_{\mathrm{ff}})$ triple}
The motor computes $k_p e + k_d \dot e + \tau_{\mathrm{ff}}$ itself, so clamping
only the \code{effort} field would not limit the torque at all. The monitor
predicts the resulting torque and, if it exceeds the budget, scales all three
terms by the same factor --- limiting the magnitude while preserving the
commanded impedance direction.
\end{decision}

The budget is the continuous rating normally, rising to peak while $I^2t$ credit
remains. The integrator charges as $(\tau/\tau_{\mathrm{cont}})^2 - 1$ above the
continuous reference, and discharges when below. This is a project protection
heuristic, not the manufacturer's exact overload curve or per-phase stall
heating model. The published one-second 36\,N$\cdot$m stall limit is not an
indefinitely repeatable pulse allowance.

\subsection{Thermal model}

\[
P_{\mathrm{cu}} = 3 I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 R_{\phi},\quad I_{\mathrm{rms}} = \tau_m/K_t,
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{th}}\,\dot T = P_{\mathrm{cu}} - \frac{T - T_{\mathrm{amb}}}{R_{\mathrm{th}}}
\]

Here $\tau_m$ is actuator-output torque before the external belt; knee joint
torque is divided by $2\times0.95$. The phase equivalent follows from the
published line resistance under an equivalent-wye assumption; thermal resistance
and capacitance remain uncalibrated. The equation uses
$R_{\mathrm{th}} = \RdRthermal$\,K/W and $C_{\mathrm{th}} = \RdCthermal$\,J/K,
giving a time constant of \RdTauThermal\,minutes. It runs in two roles: in
simulation it synthesises a plausible temperature, because MuJoCo has no concept
of copper loss and the safety architecture must be exercised; on the real robot
it runs as an \emph{observer} alongside the reported temperature, and the
monitor takes the maximum of the two. The feedback
temperature is not an identified winding measurement; sensor location and lag
must be checked on delivered hardware. Taking the maximum does not make an
uncalibrated observer a verified protection model. This single lump does not
reproduce uneven per-phase heating with a stationary current vector at stall.

Between \RdTempDerate\ and \RdTempFault\,$^\circ$C the torque budget is scaled
down linearly rather than cut at a threshold, because cutting output abruptly
would drop the robot.

\subsection{Watchdog and emergency stop}

\begin{decision}{A stale command holds position; it does not go limp}
Losing torque mid-stance drops a 28\,kg loaded robot. The watchdog substitutes an
impedance command that holds the measured position at moderate gain. If a joint
is measured outside its soft range, the hold target is the limit, so the
watchdog recovers the envelope gently rather than freezing outside it.
\end{decision}

A communication timeout on any joint latches an e-stop: a silently stale joint
is the failure mode most likely to break a leg.

\subsection{Package-level architecture in depth}

The workspace is deliberately split by ownership boundary rather than by file
type. \code{robodog\_msgs} is the only package allowed to be imported by every
layer. Its aggregate \code{RobotState} message carries the full coherent robot
snapshot, while services such as \code{SetGait}, \code{EnableJoints},
\code{EmergencyStop} and \code{SaveMap} describe the small set of state-changing
operations exposed outside the controller. This keeps GUI, logger and mobile
code from depending on Python implementation classes.

\code{robodog\_description} owns static robot facts: generated Xacro, meshes,
actuator YAML, named poses, joint limits and mass budget. \code{robodog\_sim}
converts the same description into MJCF and world geometry, then supplies the
MuJoCo backend and RViz world markers. \code{robodog\_hardware} owns
motor/joint conversions, RS06 CAN framing, backend registration and telemetry
types. \code{robodog\_control} owns every command decision that can move a
joint: the gait generator, balance law, state estimator, safety monitor and
single control loop. \code{robodog\_perception} owns camera backends,
PointCloud2 generation, mapping launch, RTAB-Map database/session planning and
room-scan export. \code{robodog\_web} bridges the ROS graph into a constrained
browser protocol. \code{robodog\_bringup} only composes these pieces.

The practical rule is simple: a lower layer may expose facts and bounded
operations, but it may not reach upward. For example, the map exporter can call
RTAB-Map backup and OctoMap saver services, but it cannot change gait state;
the GUI can request \code{/robodog/mapping/save\_map}, but map export cannot
write directly into controller internals. This is why the same launch file can
turn mapping on for MuJoCo and leave it disabled for hardware without changing
the controller.

\subsection{The hardware boundary}
\label{sec:boundary}

\archfig{hardware_boundary}{The command and telemetry contracts survive the
swap to hardware. Hardware sensor integration remains outstanding; optional
mapping is disabled there. The direction and offset map translates motor and
canonical joint coordinates.}{1.0}

\code{JointBackend} declares configuration, command, feedback and base-state methods:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
class JointBackend(ABC):
    name: str; is_simulation: bool
    def configure(self) -> None          # open devices / load model
    def enable(self, mask) -> None       # energise
    def disable(self, mask) -> None      # de-energise, back-driveable
    def read(self) -> JointState         # pos, vel, effort, temperature, faults
    def write(self, cmd: JointCommand)   # already safety-clamped
    def step(self, dt) -> None           # advance a simulator; no-op on hardware
    def base_state(self) -> BaseState | None   # None on real hardware
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{decision}{\code{base\_state()} returns \code{None} on the real robot}
A simulator knows the floating-base state exactly; the real robot has no sensor
for absolute base pose. Rather than inventing one, the contract makes the
absence explicit and forces every consumer to handle it. Real IMU integration
is currently missing, so hardware travel is refused rather than synthesizing
gravity and zero gyro samples. The estimator design below describes a future
integration path; it does not establish a working hardware balance loop.
\end{decision}

Three implementations exist. \code{KinematicBackend} models each joint as an
independent second-order system with the reflected rotor inertia --- no physics
engine, no contact, no gravity --- so core control can be exercised without a
physics engine. Mapping is disabled in this mode. \code{MujocoBackend} adds
rigid-body dynamics and contact. \code{RobStride06Backend} drives the real
motors.

\section{Simulation model and test environments}

\archfig{simulation_architecture}{The MJCF and the URDF are generated from the
same parameters, so the physics and the kinematics cannot drift apart.}{1.0}

MuJoCo advances generalized coordinates $q$ and velocities $v$ under the
multibody equation
\[
  M(q)\dot v+c(q,v)=\tau+J_c(q)^{\mathsf T}f_c,
\]
where $M$ is the mass matrix, $c$ collects bias terms, $\tau$ includes applied
actuator generalized force and $J_c^{\mathsf T}f_c$ represents constraint and
contact forces~\cite{mujoco-paper,mujoco-computation}. RoboDog records the
actuator contribution \code{qfrc\_actuator}; it does not label total contact or
constraint reaction as motor torque.

\subsection{Fidelity choices}

These are not convenience settings; each is here because getting it wrong yields
a controller that works in simulation and fails on the robot.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The impedance law is evaluated at the physics rate}, not the
      control rate. The real RS06 closes its loop in firmware at tens of kHz;
      holding a \RdControlRate\,Hz zero-order hold on $k_p(q^*-q)$ would make the
      simulated joints noticeably softer than the hardware and would tune gains
      that are wrong on the robot. Only the \emph{setpoint} is held.
\item \textbf{\code{armature} carries equivalent output inertia}:
      0.012\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$ for direct hips and 0.048\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$
      after the external knee reduction. Omitting it overstates acceleration.
\item \textbf{Command latency is injected} --- two physics steps, about 1\,ms,
      the order of a 1\,Mbit/s bus at \RdControlRate\,Hz. Controllers tuned
      against a zero-latency plant are optimistic in the wrong direction.
\item \textbf{Capsules, not cylinders}, for the leg segments. URDF has no
      capsule primitive; MuJoCo does, and capsule contact is far better
      conditioned than the flat-ended cylinder a URDF must approximate it with.
\item \textbf{Timestep 0.5\,ms} (2\,kHz), exactly five substeps per control
      cycle. A non-integer ratio would make the effective command latency jitter.
\end{enumerate}

Not modelled: gearbox backlash, belt compliance in the knee drive, current-loop
bandwidth, and CAN jitter.

\subsection{Validation against the model}

The current tests compare generated URDF and MJCF masses, origins, axes and
joint limits and verify unobstructed hip motion. At rest, total ground reaction
should approach $m g$; for the loaded 28.000\,kg model that is approximately
274.68\,N. Equal load sharing would be about 68.67\,N per foot, but the
actual front/rear split depends on the generated center of mass and stance.
Current simulated displacement, tilt and per-motor loads are recorded in
\href{rs06_28kg_1ms_100s.md}{docs/rs06\_28kg\_1ms\_100s.md}, with JSON, CSV and
MuJoCo XML exports;
\href{locomotion_validation.md}{the locomotion validation report} summarizes
failed speeds, turn-rate errors and the shared-controller Go2 comparison.
The earlier 10\,kg validation numbers are not evidence for this configuration.

\subsection{Test environment}

\begin{decision}{One world description, two renderers}
\code{world\_spec.py} is consumed by the MJCF builder and by the RViz marker
publisher. What the operator sees in RViz is geometrically what the robot
collides with in MuJoCo; two hand-maintained files would drift, and the drift
would look like a perception bug.
\end{decision}

Both worlds include a static standing human mannequin, exactly 1.90\,m from
soles to crown, beside the robot's spawn and clear of its forward travel lane.
Its feet rest on the floor; the same primitive geometry appears in MuJoCo and
RViz. It provides a size reference rather than a human dynamics model. The
\href{images/human_reference_flat.png}{flat-world} and
\href{images/human_reference_house.png}{house} images show the comparison.

Two worlds ship. \code{flat} is an open-air proving ground, 24\,$\times$\,24\,m,
with eight independent lanes radiating from the spawn so that each locomotion
problem can be attempted on its own: a measured run with metre markers for
velocity tuning, a step ladder from 40 to 200\,mm, a stair complex (six
80\,mm steps up, a landing, six down, and a 130\,mm flight beside it), slopes
at 5, 10, 15 and 20$^\circ$, a 196-tile rough-terrain field, stepping stones,
a gap course widening from 100 to 280\,mm, and a 220\,mm balance beam with a
pole slalom. Gait work should not be debugged against furniture at the same
time, which is why it is separate from the house.

\code{house} is 12\,$\times$\,9\,m and contains every indoor locomotion
problem the robot must solve, each with enough approach room to be attempted in
isolation:

\section{Perception, SLAM and three-dimensional mapping}

\archfig{perception_pipeline}{Camera and depth pipeline. Simulated calibration
is derived from the renderer; the real camera still requires calibration and
depth integration. Mapping uses registered RGB and depth.}{1.0}

The Yahboom NUWA HP60C is mounted on the body's front face on a bracket at
$z = 175$\,mm, pitched 0.26\,rad down.

\begin{decision}{Historical camera mount study}
The original camera-mount study predates the revised RS06 geometry and mapping
calibration. In that study the front hip assemblies occupied roughly
$x = 0.11 \ldots 0.26$\,m and reached
$z = +0.05$\,m, leaving only a 42\,mm slot between them on the centreline.
Rendering the simulated depth image from $z = 20$\,mm put \textbf{95\,\%} of the
frame on the robot's own legs at 74\,mm; $z = 90$\,mm still left 48\,\%. Clearing
the hip corner with the bottom edge of the frustum requires $z > 155$\,mm, so
the selected mount was raised. At 175\,mm the recorded self-view was
0\,\% and 98.5\,\% of the depth image was usable. These percentages are
historical results, not fresh measurements of the current model.
\end{decision}

\begin{decision}{The simulated camera loads its own MuJoCo model}
The physics instance lives in the control node's backend. Rather than reach into
it, the camera backend loads the same scene read-only and drives it from the
published robot state. The camera has a separate node and lifecycle, keeping
rendering outside the control callback and camera failures isolated from the
control process. Rendering and mapping still compete for workstation resources;
the mapping runs below operated slower than real time.
\end{decision}

Depth crosses the backend boundary as float32 metres with NaN for invalid,
because that representation can express ``no reading''; the conversion to the
ROS 16UC1-millimetre convention (where 0 means invalid) happens once, at the
publisher. Stereo range error grows as $z^2$, so the simulated camera applies
$\sigma_z = z^2 \sigma_d / (f b)$ --- about 4\,mm at 1\,m and 140\,mm at 6\,m ---
and the point cloud is filtered to \code{max\_usable\_range\_m} rather than the
datasheet maximum.

For a registered depth sample $z(u,v)$ and calibrated intrinsics
$(f_x,f_y,c_x,c_y)$, the organized point cloud uses the pinhole inverse
projection
\[
  X=(u-c_x)z/f_x,\qquad Y=(v-c_y)z/f_y,\qquad Z=z.
\]
Invalid or out-of-range pixels are omitted. The optical frame follows REP-103:
$Z$ forward, $X$ right and $Y$ down; TF places each observation into the map
frame. Registration, matching timestamps and one optical origin are therefore
part of the geometry, not presentation metadata.

\begin{caveat}{Camera parameters are placeholders}
Resolution, field of view, range and baseline in
\code{config/nuwa\_hp60c.yaml} are plausible for a device of this class but have
\emph{not} been read from the datasheet; each is marked \code{[VERIFY]}. The
simulated camera instead publishes calibration derived from its rendered field
of view and image dimensions; this does not validate the physical device.
How the HP60C delivers depth over USB (a second UVC
stream, a side-by-side frame, or only through the vendor SDK) is the one thing
that cannot be determined without the device, and is marked \code{HW-CHECK} in
\code{backends/nuwa\_hp60c.py}.
\end{caveat}

\subsection{RGB-D SLAM and 3D occupancy}
\label{sec:mapping}

SLAM estimates a trajectory and corrects the map when places are revisited;
OctoMap represents occupied, free and unknown 3D space using those poses.
RTAB-Map supplies both the SLAM graph and its built-in OctoMap here, without a
separate OctoMap mapping server. The default \code{mapping:=auto} enables it
for \code{backend:=mujoco}, \code{camera\_backend:=sim} and
\code{use\_camera:=true}. It disables mapping for the other sensor pipelines.
\code{mapping:=none} disables processing explicitly; \code{mapping:=rtabmap}
requires a supported configuration and fails early otherwise.

\paragraph{Why RTAB-Map and OctoMap are both present.} They solve different
parts of the problem. RTAB-Map is the SLAM system: it consumes synchronized
RGB, registered depth, camera calibration and odometry, builds a graph of
observations, and can correct the graph when the robot revisits a place.
OctoMap is the volumetric occupancy representation: it stores probabilistic 3D
free/occupied/unknown space in an octree. Running an independent accumulating
OctoMap mapper beside graph SLAM would make loop-closure corrections ambiguous,
so this stack uses the OctoMap products assembled by RTAB-Map's map manager.

RTAB-Map associates each accepted observation with a pose-graph node. Visual
features propose loop closures, geometric registration constrains candidate
links, and graph optimization can revise older poses~\cite{rtabmap-paper}.
The database retains this graph and its sensor observations. It is richer than
an occupancy export because later optimization can rebuild map products from
corrected node poses.

OctoMap stores occupancy in an adaptive octree. For voxel $n$, a binary sensor
model updates log odds rather than overwriting a Boolean state:
\[
 L_t(n)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
 L_{t-1}(n)+\log\frac{p(n\mid z_t)}{1-p(n\mid z_t)}-L_0,
 L_{\min},L_{\max}\right),
 \qquad p_t(n)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-L_t(n)}}.
\]
Ray tracing marks traversed cells as free and endpoint cells as occupied;
unobserved cells remain unknown~\cite{octomap}. The 5\,cm cell size controls
map discretization and memory use. It does not improve camera accuracy or SLAM
pose accuracy.

\paragraph{Frame ownership.} Control publishes \code{odom}$\to$\code{base\_link}
because the walking controller owns base feedback. RTAB-Map is configured with
\code{frame\_id=base\_link}, \code{odom\_frame\_id=odom} and
\code{map\_frame\_id=map}; it publishes \code{map}$\to$\code{odom}. This
matches the ROS mobile-base convention: \code{odom} is locally continuous and
may drift, while \code{map} is globally corrected and may jump. The mapper is
therefore not a replacement for the state estimator used by control.

Mapping mode renders registered $640\times480$ RGB and depth from one optical
origin. Both images and their calibration use
\code{camera\_color\_optical\_frame} and the acquisition timestamp of the
robot state used to render them. Intrinsics follow the rendered camera's
vertical field of view and image dimensions. ROS depth remains
\code{16UC1} millimetres. Matching best-effort QoS and exact synchronization
preserve this camera contract; repeated stale poses are not republished.

The initial map settings are 5\,cm cells, a 4\,m depth range and 2\,Hz
processing. Cell size is resolution, not sensor accuracy. The namespace
\code{/robodog/mapping} contains \code{map} (2D occupancy),
\code{octomap\_occupied\_space} (3D points), \code{octomap\_full},
\code{octomap\_binary} and \code{mapData}. Outputs are generated when
subscribed; the map cache remains when viewers disconnect. Mapping RViz uses
the \code{map} frame and shows both 2D and 3D occupancy.

\paragraph{Persistence and export.} Each launch creates a preserved,
timestamped database for its world:
\begin{lstlisting}
$ROS_HOME/robodog/maps/<world>-<UTC timestamp>.db
# When ROS_HOME is unset:
~/.ros/robodog/maps/<world>-<UTC timestamp>.db
\end{lstlisting}
To deliberately continue or inspect a specific file without deleting any
session, pass this launch argument:
\begin{lstlisting}
mapping_database:=/absolute/path/session.db
\end{lstlisting}
The \code{/robodog/mapping/rtabmap/backup} service flushes a \code{.back} copy.

The official \code{octomap\_saver\_node} exports a full colored
\code{ColorOcTree} to \code{.ot} via the node's \code{octomap\_full} service;
the database retains the graph and observations, whereas \code{.ot} is an
occupancy snapshot. Exact service remapping and installation commands are in
\href{mapping.md}{the mapping runbook}.

The room-scan service wraps these pieces into one immutable export directory:
\code{cloud\_map.pcd} from \topicpath{/robodog/mapping/cloud_map},
\code{octomap.ot} from
\topicpath{/robodog/mapping/rtabmap/octomap_full},
\code{rtabmap.db.back} from
\topicpath{/robodog/mapping/rtabmap/backup}, and a manifest with byte counts
and SHA-256 checksums. It refuses stale clouds, relative output roots and
existing session names. The export is therefore a scan artifact that can be
copied, inspected or archived without mutating the live SLAM database.

\paragraph{Validation and scope.} Recorded 0.500\,m house and 0.501\,m flat
walks grew occupied point clouds from 687 to 1,053 and 1,837 to 4,163 points,
respectively. Final real-time factors were 0.608 and 0.617. House database
reload retained 523 known 2D cells before moving; stored observations increased
from 18 to 24 after restart. Full exports contained 7,964 house and 27,567 flat
tree nodes, a different count from occupied points. These short integration
checks acknowledged stop/stand commands and observed no clamp events during
their measurement window; they do not supersede the later failed post-walk
stand checks. \href{mapping_validation.json}{The mapping record} preserves
the measurements and export hashes.

MuJoCo supplies exact odometry, so these runs do not establish real visual
odometry accuracy or loop-closure performance. Kinematic mode has no moving
base odometry or world spawn placement. The real NUWA backend does not yet
provide depth, and real base odometry is not integrated. Hardware mapping needs
calibrated registered depth and a validated odometry source with exclusive
ownership of \code{odom}$\to$\code{base\_link}. This integration does not add
autonomous navigation or obstacle avoidance.

\section{Operator interfaces and telemetry}

A single process holds an rclpy node and an aiohttp server; rclpy spins in a
background thread while aiohttp owns the asyncio loop. They meet at two
dictionaries: the ROS callbacks write the latest state, the web tasks read it.
The browser receives the newest snapshot rather than a backlog. A separate,
bounded server-side history computes diagnostics from each received telemetry
sample before display throttling.

\begin{decision}{A purpose-built protocol rather than rosbridge}
rosbridge would re-serialise every field at the full publish rate and expose the
whole graph to the browser. This speaks a small versioned JSON protocol,
decimated to 20\,Hz, and exposes exactly the commands the GUI is allowed to
send. Less code, less bandwidth, and a much smaller surface from a page that can
drive a robot.
\end{decision}

The browser fetches \code{/api/config} and builds itself from it: adding,
removing or reordering a panel is a change to \code{config/web.yaml} alone.
Panels cover robot state, controller state, per-joint telemetry with tracking
error and load bars, foot contact and forces, safety and thermal state,
simulation state and real-time factor, an event log, camera streams, pose and
gait commands, and emergency stop. Expanded robot information describes the
configured mass, geometry and actuator/transmission limits; it is not a
measurement of the assembled machine.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/joint_diagnostics.png}
\caption{Live joint diagnostics with rolling torque, thermal and tracking
summaries. The displayed values are a mixed-motion interface check, not a
steady-gait motor-sizing result.}
\label{fig:joint-diagnostics}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Joint load and history conventions}

Each of the twelve joints reports live position, velocity, tracking error,
signed joint and motor torque, estimated current and temperature. The rolling
20-second summary adds joint/motor RMS and absolute peak torque, motor speed
at that peak sample, exposure above the 8\,N$\cdot$m continuous-stall and
11\,N$\cdot$m rotating references, maximum temperature, tracking-error RMS
and mean signed mechanical power. Exposure is descriptive, not an allowed
overload duration or prediction of failure.

The RS06 internal gearbox is already included in ``motor-output torque.'' For
the external knee belt, $g=2\times0.95=1.9$, so
$\tau_m=\tau_j/1.9$ and $\dot q_m=2\dot q_j$; the hips have no external
reduction. A feed-forward command is only one term of impedance control, so
its difference from feedback is not a clipping measurement.

The server normally receives telemetry at 50\,Hz and updates the browser at
20\,Hz. It integrates a sample over its following valid interval:
\[
 \tau_{\mathrm{RMS}}=
 \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i\tau_i^2\,\Delta t_i}{\sum_i\Delta t_i}}.
\]
MuJoCo statistics use simulation time; other backends use message timestamps.
Thus a slow renderer does not inflate exposure. Partial windows show their
valid coverage. Gaps over 0.25\,s or invalid joint data restart coverage;
duplicate and slightly reversed timestamps are rejected. A reversal over one
second or clock-source change resets the timeline. The GUI reports these
events and leaves missing history unavailable. Browser refresh preserves the
server history; server restart resets it. Peaks are telemetry-sampled and can
miss contact impacts between messages; the physics-step sizing reports remain
the appropriate source for those peaks.

\paragraph{Source provenance.} MuJoCo effort is applied physics actuator torque
sampled into telemetry; CAN effort is actuator feedback; kinematic effort is
synthetic. Current is a torque/$K_t$ equivalent phase-RMS estimate, not measured
phase, battery or $i_q$ current, and excludes high-current saturation.
Simulation temperature is an uncalibrated copper-loss model; CAN uses the
maximum of reported temperature and its thermal observer. Bus voltage is an
assumption and electrical power an estimate. Mechanical power is signed
$\tau_j\dot q_j$, not battery power. Clamp counts are aggregate command-limiter
events, not per-joint actuator clipping. These distinctions are retained in
the display and explained in \href{gui_telemetry.md}{the diagnostics guide}.

Operator limits are configuration, not constants in JavaScript: maximum
commanded velocity, gait confirmation, and joint jogging --- which is disabled
by default because it bypasses the gait layer and is the easiest way to put a
leg where the body cannot support it. Video is a separate MJPEG endpoint so a
slow video consumer cannot stall telemetry, and the page falls back to showing
camera metadata if no JPEG encoder is installed.

The simulation-only Greeting action is a queued whole-body motion. One request
first selects stand and waits for finite feedback, four contacts, stand height,
level/stationary base feedback and settled joints. It then moves the base
rear-right, lifts and waves the front-left paw, touches down, and only then
recentres. Kinematic regression tests require at least 40\,mm support-triangle
margin while paw clearance exceeds 60\,mm and bound commanded joint speed to
1.25\,rad/s. A live 8.70\,s MuJoCo run returned to standing with minimum base
height 0.294\,m, maximum absolute roll 0.067\,rad and maximum absolute pitch
0.021\,rad. This establishes simulation behavior only; the service still
rejects hardware. The reproducible command and full-precision record are in
\href{greeting_validation.json}{the greeting validation artifact}.

\subsection{LAN discovery and mobile application}

The server publishes a read-only \code{\_robodog.\_tcp.local} DNS-SD service
when \code{avahi-publish-service} and a running Avahi daemon are available. The
service instance carries the friendly name; its TXT records contain a stable UUID,
model, API version and the path
\code{/.well-known/robodog}. The JSON descriptor repeats the identity and lists
the state, MJPEG and web-remote paths. The UUID is created once below
\code{\$ROS\_HOME/robodog/device.json} with mode 0600; it is an identifier,
not a secret. Discovery can be disabled with \code{web\_discovery:=false}, and
manual IP/port entry remains available where multicast is filtered.

The React Native application lives outside the ROS workspace in
\code{apps/robodog\_mobile}. Its network boundary validates discovery and
telemetry payloads before they enter application state, deduplicates robots by
the stable UUID, and keeps motion controls separate from discovery. Phone
portrait keeps camera and telemetry above a bottom control area with two
side-by-side joysticks. Phone landscape places MOVE and TURN around the live
camera and keeps dead-man status plus compact action buttons beside the thumbs. Mode, gait, measured
speed, configured supply-voltage assumption, hottest-joint temperature and
rolling torque RMS/peak remain visible.

The cockpit's joysticks with touch dead-man behavior, Stand, Walk, Greeting and
emergency stop are active through a hidden, navigation-pinned WebView at
\code{/remote?native\_bridge=1}. The robot-served page opens \code{/ws}, so
the browser Origin and Host exactly match the selected robot. The opt-in bridge
requires both the query flag and React Native message channel, accepts a strict
finite command schema, and returns validated status and telemetry messages. It
does not create a standalone native socket or relax the hostname allowlist.
Commands retain the server's one-operator 0.30\,s lease and the control node's
independent 0.35\,s velocity timeout. Dead-man release, backgrounding and view
teardown send zero; disconnect or lease expiry also produces zero. A visible
Web Remote remains available for the complete browser interface. Expo Go can
use manual address entry; automatic DNS-SD discovery requires the included
Swift/Kotlin module in a development build. Setup and operator details are in the
\href{mobile_app.md}{mobile app runbook}.

\begin{decision}{Discovery does not confer control authority}
The service advertisement and well-known descriptor are public LAN metadata.
They do not add a native command bypass or relax the existing \code{/ws}
Origin, Host or private-network checks. The hidden WebView bridge deliberately
uses those existing checks. The shipped HTTP/MJPEG/web-control path has no
login or TLS and is therefore restricted to an isolated trusted LAN. A
production control layer must add TLS, pinned robot identity, a time-limited
pairing code, revocable per-device credentials, strict message schemas, rate
limits and a lease covering every motion-changing action.
\end{decision}

The present camera path is not a 60-fps path: ROS publishes the simulated
camera at 15\,Hz and the Python MJPEG encoder is configured for at most 10\,Hz
at 640 pixels wide. A later low-latency feed belongs behind a separate media
transport using Jetson hardware H.264 and WebRTC with native decoding. Camera
sensor output, encoder throughput, decoded frame rate, latency, packet loss,
CPU/GPU load and temperature must be measured before a 60-fps profile is
advertised. Command telemetry remains on a separate transport so video
congestion cannot delay dead-man messages.

\subsection{Experimental protocol and acceptance criteria}

Both 28\,kg cases start from the same standing keyframe on an obstacle-free

the first 5\,s; peaks, threshold exposure and clipping include startup. These

interval is available.

\subsection{Research and evidence traceability}
\label{sec:research-basis}

RoboDog is not a finished product; it is a research and bring-up platform whose
most important deliverable is a traceable model-to-controller chain. The
architecture contributes five concrete pieces:

\begin{enumerate}
\item a generated mechanical description in which CAD, URDF, MuJoCo, inverse
      kinematics and documentation share one parameter source;
\item a backend boundary that lets the same controller drive ideal kinematics,
      MuJoCo dynamics or RS06 CAN hardware;
\item a foot-trajectory gait controller with force-distribution feed-forward,
      motor-side load accounting and per-cycle safety limiting;
\item an RGB-D mapping pipeline that uses RTAB-Map for graph SLAM and its
      graph-corrected OctoMap products for room scanning;
\item a measurement method that reports applied physics torque, motor-side RMS,

\paragraph{Experimental method.} A feasible operating point must complete the
run, stay upright, track commanded body velocity, keep worst motor RMS below
the configured continuous-stall reference and avoid meaningful command
intervention by the safety limiter. RMS is computed after warmup as
\[
  \tau_{\mathrm{RMS}} =
  \sqrt{\frac{\int_{t_0}^{T}\tau_m(t)^2\,dt}{T-t_0}},
\]
where $\tau_m$ is actuator-output torque before any external knee belt. Peak,
time above 8/11/36\,N$\cdot$m, speed at peak and clipping durations include
startup because startup is part of the commanded maneuver. That separation is
intentional: RMS estimates heating tendency, while peak and exposure describe
shock, overload and limiter stress.

\section{Discussion, limitations and conclusions}

\subsection{Interpretation of the 28 kg experiment}

The walk result is a controller failure: the body exceeds the tilt and height
limits, reverses net travel and spends essentially the entire run under safety
intervention. Its RMS values include the unstable and fallen interval and must
not be interpreted as the load of a successful 1.0\,m/s walk.

The trot result is more informative. It remains upright and covers 92.227\,m,
but its 0.922506\,m/s mean speed misses the tracking threshold and the safety
monitor changes commands during 87.965\,\% of cycles. Added mass reduces speed
by 0.061\,m/s relative to the 19.719271\,kg case, increases the worst motor RMS
by 1.389\,N$\cdot$m, increases the peak by 6.259\,N$\cdot$m and raises limiter
activity by 57.3 percentage points. The largest peak occurs at 0.681\,s on the
rear-left hip pitch joint, during the acceleration transient. A speed ramp and
transition-specific gain scheduling are therefore higher-value next tests than
another identical steady command.

The 2:1 knee transmission reduces inferred knee motor torque by $r\eta=1.9$
and doubles motor speed, which is favorable for the reported knee RMS. The
same transmission reflects actuator inertia by $r^2=4$ and introduces belt,
pulley, shaft, tension and compliance questions that the rigid, 95\,\%-efficient
simulation does not answer. Upright simulated motion therefore cannot qualify
the mechanical transmission.

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\begin{itemize}
\item No physical motor, battery, CAN bus, IMU, camera, belt or structure has
      been exercised. Direction, zero offsets, cooling and supply behavior are
      unverified.
\item The 8.280729\,kg ballast is centered and adds mass without a measured
      payload location or inertia tensor. A real payload changes pitch/roll
      inertia and front/rear foot loading.
\item Belt efficiency, compliance, backlash, gearbox friction, motor current
      loop dynamics, battery sag and regeneration are absent or assumed.
\item The first-order copper-loss model has uncalibrated thermal resistance and
      capacitance. Reported temperatures are illustrative estimates, not a
      winding-temperature validation.
\item The current 28\,kg evidence comprises deterministic flat-ground single
      runs. It provides no repeatability statistics, terrain envelope or
      confidence interval.
\item MuJoCo contact, friction and damping are model choices. Physics success
      does not remove sim-to-real contact or latency error.
\item Mapping uses simulator ground-truth \code{odom}$\to$\code{base\_link}.
      The checks validate ROS integration, map growth, reload and export; they
      do not measure visual-odometry drift, loop-closure precision or room-scale
      trajectory error.
\item Physical NUWA depth delivery and calibration remain unknown, autonomous
      navigation and obstacle avoidance are not implemented, and the current
      camera path is 15\,Hz ROS/10\,Hz MJPEG rather than 60\,fps.
\item The Go2 study applies the RoboDog controller to the official model. It is
      a plant comparison, not a comparison with Unitree's factory controller.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion and prioritized work}

The supported conclusion is that RoboDog has a traceable model, a coherent ROS
2 boundary, an inspectable locomotion controller, physics-rate joint-load
metrics and a persistent simulated mapping pipeline. The unsupported
conclusion is that the 28\,kg robot can reliably walk or trot at 1.0\,m/s with
comfortable ROBSTRIDE06 margin. The present acceptance result is a failure.

The next work should measure the real mass distribution, belt efficiency and
actuator thermal response; replace the step start with a bounded speed ramp and
retune transitions; integrate and validate IMU/contact base estimation; run
repeated seeds and terrain cases; calibrate registered camera depth; and only
then perform staged single-joint, suspended-leg, stand and low-speed hardware
commissioning. Each stage must retain motor-output RMS, peak, overload duration,
speed at peak, limiter intervention and fault provenance.

\subsection{Bring-up sequence}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Verify the CAN protocol.} Power one motor alone; confirm
      \code{GET\_DEVICE\_ID}, then \code{ENABLE} and the arrival of feedback
      frames, then a 0.1\,N$\cdot$m command with $k_p = k_d = 0$ and check the
      direction of motion. A firmware revision with a different identifier
      layout changes exactly one file.
\item \textbf{Calibrate each joint.} \code{ros2 run robodog\_hardware
      calibrate\_joint} determines \code{direction} and \code{offset\_rad}
      against the convention in Section~\ref{sec:conventions} and prints a YAML
      fragment for review. Leave \code{enable\_on\_start: false} until signed off.
\item \textbf{Validate the remaining assumptions.} Check assembly dynamics
      against the published output-equivalent inertia; identify friction, damping
      and cooling through controlled measurements within manufacturer limits.
      The 11\,N$\cdot$m rotating rating is not a continuous stall allowance.
      Until then the margins in
      Section~\ref{sec:safety} carry the uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Bring up on a stand}, legs free: joint test sweeps, then named
      poses, then stance with the body supported.
\item \textbf{On the ground}, walk gait first --- it is statically stable and
      keeps three feet down.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Planned next steps}

The \code{ros2\_control} interface contract is already declared in
\code{urdf/ros2\_control.xacro} but not implemented: the runtime is Python.

\begin{decision}{Python control loop now, C++ \code{SystemInterface} later}
At \RdControlRate\,Hz the measured loop period is 2.49\,ms against a nominal
2.50\,ms, with worst-case jitter under 1\,ms --- adequate, and it buys a stack
that is fully testable and quick to change during architecture work. The
declared \code{ros2\_control} block is the formal statement of the interfaces a
C++ \code{SystemInterface} must provide, and is the migration path once jitter
or CPU headroom demands it. Note that the command interface set there is the
RS06's native impedance command, not a single position interface, for the reason
in Section~\ref{sec:actuator}.
\end{decision}

\section{Reproducibility and verification}

The latest recorded regression run on 28 September 2026 passed all 364 tests.
The statistics module has 100\,\% statement coverage, including irregular
timing, belt conversion, partial windows, invalid samples, resets and bounded
history; browser tests keep unavailable values distinct from zero. Camera
tests reconstruct the rendered floor using published calibration. Live Chrome
checks populated a full 20-second history at approximately 50\,Hz without
browser errors or telemetry gaps and verified default mapping in both worlds.
The separate fault-free post-walk stand assertion failed with
\code{TORQUE\_LIMIT}; passing regression tests do not resolve that load issue.
All launched integration-test nodes exited cleanly.

Run the suite again for a changed checkout. The key checks cover URDF/MJCF
consistency, joint-to-motor conversion, CAN scaling, bounded commands, free hip
motion, continuous gait targets and actual simulated displacement. Tests must
compare knee motor load after the belt conversion, not directly against joint
torque. Record the command, test count and skips with each feasibility report.

The most valuable are the cross-checks: the MJCF's per-link masses, joint
origins, axes and ranges are asserted against the URDF, so the two descriptions
cannot drift. Seven tests exist specifically because a bug got past a weaker
one: the gravity-torque sign, the idle-command rate-limiter seed, the camera
self-occlusion fraction, the Euler-convention mismatch that left the rendered
legs beside the body while every physics test passed, the swing arc's
discontinuity at lift-off, the self-collision that welded the front hips, and
the gait's thermal load.

The last three describe historical RS02 regression work. That thermal test
spent most of the project as an expected failure at 173\,\% of continuous, and it did not turn
green by being tuned --- it turned green when the two bugs above it were
fixed, and that historical run read 90\,\%. An expected-failure test is a good way to hold
a known gap where a counter will find it, but it says nothing about
\emph{why} the gap exists: the reason sat two layers below where the test
pointed, in the simulation model.

\subsection{Capability envelope}
\label{sec:capability}

The current \code{capability\_report.py} provides a static point-load screen
with the active mass and per-joint belt conversion. It assumes equal supporting
foot loads and omits limb gravity, acceleration and asymmetric contacts. It
therefore does not certify payload, stair climbing, slope traction, jumps or
landings. Reach reserve is reported as geometry, not as demonstrated step
height. Use the full gait report and dedicated terrain experiments for dynamic
claims. Thermal feasibility also requires identified cooling on the assembled
robot; the estimated copper-loss model is insufficient by itself.
